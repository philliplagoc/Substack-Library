no ads, no paid promotion nor any third party manipulation (with proof).

I’ve grown over 3,000 subscribers in my first month on Substack. No ads, no third-party shoutout groups, no cross-promo swaps in some Discord where everyone plugs each other’s newsletters and calls it growth. In this newsletter I will be showing you proof of that later.

It’s just been me, a blank page, and Notes. I want to walk you through how that actually happened, because the real story isn’t as clean as “I posted good content and people showed up.” It took me almost a full week to even understand what game I was playing and growth was slow.

With that being said and the knowledge I have acquired, we could be looking at 5,000+ next month.

Note: I’ve been ghostwriting for 3 years for accounts and builders, over 700k followers have been added to clients via on X. this wasn’t luck, this is experience.

When I started, I didn’t feel like a beginner, because I understood human psychology and have been studying it for over half a decade.

When I started, I made the same assumption most people make when they move to this platform. I figured the newsletter itself would do the heavy lifting. When it comes to paid subs, yes this is true, but not your overall growth.

Write something worth reading, hit publish, watch the subscriber count climb on its own. That’s how it works everywhere else, more or less but it’s not that simple.

You put out good work and the algorithm or the word of mouth carries it. So that’s what I did for the first few days. I wrote two newsletters I was genuinely proud of, shared them everywhere I could think of, and sat back to watch what happened.

The magic is 11 subscribers… not 1,000, not even 100.

I remember opening the dashboard on day four and just staring at that number for a second, waiting for it to feel like more than it was. Eleven people, after two newsletters and every ounce of effort I had. I’d put real work into those essays. Hours of writing and editing, a title I’d rewritten probably a dozen times, and the return on all of it was eleven strangers deciding to trust me with their inbox. It was hard not to read that as a verdict on the whole idea.

So I texted a friend who’d been on the platform longer than me and told him it wasn’t working. He didn’t try to hype me up or tell me to keep grinding on the same thing. He just said I was playing a different game than I thought I was, and that I should give it two weeks before deciding anything. That stuck with me more than I expected it to, mostly because I didn’t understand it yet.

It took me a few more days of flat numbers to actually get what he meant. I’d been treating Substack like a publishing platform, a place you post finished work and wait for it to find an audience. But that’s not really what it is, or at least it’s not only that. Underneath the newsletter layer, it’s a social network, with its own feed, its own discovery mechanism, and its own behavior that has almost nothing to do with how good your essays are. The newsletter is the storefront. Notes is the street it sits on. And I’d been polishing the storefront while ignoring the fact that nobody was walking down that street yet.

Once that clicked, the whole month changed. Not overnight, and not because I found some clever trick. It changed because I started showing up on the part of the platform actually built for people to find you, instead of assuming the writing alone would do that job for me.
